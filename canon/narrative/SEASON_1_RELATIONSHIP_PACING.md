# Season 1 Relationship Pacing — Nathan and Alex

Status: WORKING PROPOSAL, consistent with established relationship intent
Updated: 2026-10-12

## Non-negotiable direction

Nathan and Alex are the intended endgame pairing, but **friends before romance**. The relationship must grow from repeated ordinary contact, shared attention, trust and mutual experience—not from the author announcing chemistry or turning every scene into flirtation.

Nathan's attraction begins early because he recognizes Alex from the series. Alex does not begin with equivalent romantic interest. She first sees a new classmate who is capable, oddly careful about how he presents himself, and useful in a specific academic situation. Her later attachment must be earned by the person he proves to be in this altered timeline, not by his intelligence alone or by the reader knowing they will end up together.

## Season 1 progression

### 16–18 September 2009 — Recognition and first practical exchange

Nathan sees Alex at school on 16 September. He recognizes her and is attracted, then finds ordinary reasons not to approach. This is an internal problem for Nathan, not a cue for Alex to notice him from across the room.

Alex initiates their first real interaction on 18 September because of the *How to Solve It* resource and the advanced-class assignment. Her immediate goal is practical. Nathan's nerves affect small choices—over-careful wording, a delayed response, or a thought that distracts him—but he can answer the actual question.

### 19–30 September — Familiarity without instant intimacy

A few short encounters turn Alex from someone he watches into someone he has actually spoken to. She remembers details and may choose to ask him a follow-up question, but she is not suddenly preoccupied with him.

The exchanges should vary. One can be practical, another mildly awkward, another unexpectedly easy. Not all need a punchline or visible romantic beat. In parallel, Nathan gets a foothold through football and school so Alex is not the only source of belonging in his new life.

### October — A friendship starts to exist on its own

Their conversations occasionally continue after the original task has ended. They can disagree about a school problem, complain about an assignment, mention an unrelated interest, or walk together because neither has a reason to hurry away. No single moment has to mean very much.

Nathan begins to initiate small things sometimes, especially after Alex has made the first conversation feel safe. That is progress even if his thoughts later underrate it. Alex's decisions should have plausible reasons beyond “the plot needs another interaction.”

### Rest of Season 1 — Accumulated trust, not a romantic payoff

The intended destination for the first season is a credible friendship with its own rhythm: they know some preferences, remember earlier conversations, feel comfortable asking for practical help, and can occasionally be quiet together. Nathan may privately over-interpret small moments; the narration should signal that this is his interpretation, not proof of Alex's feelings.

Alex can gradually become more attentive to Nathan, but the pace remains restrained and sometimes ambiguous. The first season should not require a confession, official relationship, manufactured jealousy triangle or an abrupt mutual realization. Later milestones should be planned when the surrounding character development and timeline justify them.

## Scene tests

For every Nathan/Alex interaction, answer privately:

1. What does each person want at the start, apart from “to interact with the other”?
2. Why does this exchange happen on this particular day?
3. What does each already know from previous encounters?
4. Is Alex reacting to something Nathan actually did, rather than being inexplicably insightful?
5. Does Nathan's self-undervaluation affect behavior in a specific way, without repeating the same apology or inner speech?
6. What small thing has changed, if anything? It is acceptable for a scene to maintain familiarity without escalating closeness.
7. Could this conversation still feel plausible if the reader did not know they were the endgame pairing?

If the only reason a scene exists is to remind the reader that Nathan likes Alex, cut it, combine it with another purpose, or let the attraction remain off-page.


## Pacing status: hypothesis to test, not a date lock

The first-season friendship target is a working pacing hypothesis, not a command that Nathan and Alex must wait until a particular year to date. There is no fixed "Year 2 romance" decision in the current canon. The long-range outline gives us something to test, while the first episodes show what the relationship actually feels like at scene level.

After drafting Episodes 1–2 (and preferably a small sample of Episode 3), assess:
- how often they have believable access to one another;
- whether each interaction changes their shared knowledge, trust or ease;
- whether initiative comes from both sides over time, not only Nathan noticing Alex;
- whether attraction becomes visible without skipping friendship, embarrassment or uncertainty;
- whether pacing feels slow because routine life is being compressed too aggressively, or slow because the relationship genuinely needs more shared experience;
- whether a stronger romantic beat would follow from established behavior or merely satisfy an outline expectation.

If the written story supports faster development, revise the season/series anchor and add the missing setup. If it supports slower development, preserve the slower pace rather than inserting a date to meet a target. The relationship should feel natural when read forward and inevitable-enough in hindsight after backpropagation—not inevitable because a calendar says so.

Any change to the relationship target must be logged with the reason, alternatives considered and affected outlines. Do not let romance outrank scene quality or the independent lives of Nathan and Alex.
