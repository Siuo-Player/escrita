# Narrative Methodology — Working Story Bible

Status: ESTABLISHED WORKING METHOD
Updated: 2026-10-08

This document records the narrative process established during the Season 1 planning pass. It is an
authoring method, not a rigid formula for the prose.

## 1. Build the world before selecting the scenes

The underlying story world is deliberately richer than the visible text.

For recurring characters, establish internally:

- schedules and normal routines;
- current goals;
- relationships;
- obligations;
- likely locations;
- private events;
- objects and their provenance;
- decisions that may affect other characters.

Then build episode threads.

Only after that do we select the scenes that create the best story.

The final prose therefore acts like an editor/director assembling a larger recorded reality:
**create the world → let lives continue → choose the most interesting scenes → edit them into a narrative.**

A detail may exist canonically without ever appearing on the page.

## 2. Episode construction pipeline

Preferred workflow:

1. character lives / schedules;
2. episode time window;
3. independent threads;
4. causal chains;
5. natural overlaps;
6. scene beats;
7. first prose pass;
8. backpropagation;
9. continuity repair;
10. narrative editing / compression;
11. analytical review when useful.

The first outline must not become a prison. If prose reveals a better causal sequence, update the
infrastructure.

## 3. Scenes are caused, not summoned

A scene should normally answer:

- Why is each important character here?
- Where did they come from?
- What were they intending to do?
- What caused the interaction?
- What changes because they met?
- What are they likely to do afterward?

Characters can still meet by chance. The preferred model is:

**routine + independent decision + geography + timing + chance**

rather than pure authorial coincidence.

Approximately 90% of recurring interactions should arise naturally from routines, prior decisions,
obligations or established geography. The remaining space can include ordinary life coincidences.

Coincidence may start a scene, but it should not be the unexplained mechanism that solves major conflicts.

## 4. Causality visible to the reader

The story can deliberately show causal links Nathan does not know.

Example pattern:

1. a character makes a small decision;
2. the reader sees it;
3. another character independently changes plans;
4. their schedules intersect;
5. a consequence reaches Nathan later.

This produces dramatic irony without turning the narration into an omniscient explanation.

The reader should often understand the beginning of a butterfly chain before Nathan does.

Not every chain needs to be explained immediately.

## 5. Backpropagation

Backpropagation is not merely retroactive foreshadowing.

When a later event needs stronger causal support, we may go backward:

- to the previous scene;
- to an earlier episode;
- to an earlier season;
- eventually to childhood/background history.

If the required cause logically predates the story, it can exist in the unseen world.

Only add an explanatory scene when the explanation materially improves the reading experience.

A later fact should feel as though it was always possible in the universe, even when the reader was not
previously told about it.

## 6. Foreshadowing model

The preferred approach is close to **causal foreshadowing rather than symbolic foreshadowing**.

We intentionally show ordinary details because they later matter.

Examples:

- someone buys an item before it is needed;
- a meal plan explains a later dinner;
- a character changes a route before a later encounter;
- a small decision changes timing and creates a later consequence;
- a recurring object first appears through believable ownership/provenance.

This can look like ordinary slice-of-life information at first.

The reader should gradually perceive the snowball.

Do not turn every detail into an obvious clue.

## 7. Prop provenance

Important recurring objects have a plausible origin.

The internal record may track:

- who owns it;
- when it was acquired;
- why it was acquired;
- where it normally lives;
- whether another character borrowed or moved it.

The final prose only exposes that history when doing so improves the scene.

The object should never appear solely because the next scene requires it.

Groceries can follow the same principle when relevant:
if a meal matters later, the household's normal shopping/meal routine should make the food's presence
causal.

## 8. Character lives continue off-page

Characters do not freeze when Nathan leaves.

A Dunphy can:

- attend school;
- work;
- shop;
- practice an instrument;
- change plans;
- argue at home;
- make a decision;

without Nathan being involved.

The episode may show none of this, part of it, or the consequence later.

The grid is therefore a record of possible reality, not a scene checklist.

## 9. Point of view and camera

Nathan is the protagonist but not the permanent camera.

The story may use:

- Nathan-centered scenes;
- general ensemble scenes;
- a character-centered scene;
- a short cutaway to another character;
- a canon family sequence;
- a consequence scene whose information Nathan does not possess.

Strict POV is not required for every scene.

The important rule is epistemic clarity:
the reader should be able to tell whether something is known, inferred, remembered, misunderstood or objectively
observed.

The Pilot is an important model. It can be told substantially in ensemble/general fashion, with cuts
between characters, while Nathan remains a parallel thread rather than the hidden centre.

## 10. Nathan's knowledge is not narrative omniscience

Nathan's memory of the show is exceptionally precise, but it is still a character's memory.

He can know:

- that an event happened;
- how a character usually behaved;
- details actually shown.

He cannot automatically know:

- hidden motives that the series never showed;
- future divergence;
- facts he never learned.

When reality changes, Nathan first tries to rationalize the mismatch.

The long arc is:

**know the characters → predict the characters → be surprised by the people → learn who they actually are.**

## 11. Dialogue method

First draft priority:

**natural conversation over efficient exposition.**

Characters may:

- hesitate;
- interrupt;
- change subjects;
- talk about trivial things;
- misunderstand;
- fail to say what they mean;
- leave information implicit.

Do not rewrite dialogue into polished exposition merely because the author knows the entire causal history.

After the first pass, inspect for:

- repeated beats;
- repetitive phrasing;
- unnecessary technical exposition;
- scenes that explain what the reader already understands.

Technical scenes may be especially vulnerable to this second-pass editing.

## 12. Nathan's social rule

Nathan is not generally awkward once conversation begins.

His main difficulty is initiation.

Therefore:

- other people frequently create the first opening;
- Nathan can become funny, open and natural once engaged;
- his hesitation is most visible before and immediately after important interactions;
- self-undervaluation makes positive signals difficult for him to accept.

The story should show this through behavior instead of repeatedly explaining it.

## 13. Relationship progression

Especially for Nathan/Alex:

**contact → recognition → remembered contact → voluntary contact → comfort → trust → attraction → commitment**

The precise pace is allowed to vary.

Do not force a predetermined dating episode.

The reader should often perceive small progress before the characters define it.

## 14. Canon integration

Canon episodes exist as genuine ensemble stories.

Nathan does not need to participate just because he remembers them.

Canon may be:

- shown substantially in parallel;
- referenced afterward;
- encountered through a natural local interaction;
- allowed to cause a consequence Nathan later experiences.

Do not turn canon appearances into plot summaries.

The fanfic's divergence should come from changed information, timing, relationships and consequences.

## 15. Pacing and compression

The internal calendar may be dense.

The prose does not have to be.

A week can be represented by:

- one complete scene;
- several short scenes;
- a transition paragraph;
- a consequence;
- a time jump.

The reader should feel that life continued while the camera was elsewhere.

## 16. Technical storyline method

Nathan's technical achievements must follow a visible learning chain.

Preferred pattern:

**knowledge/reading → hypothesis → implementation → measurement → failure/limitation → modification →
test → integration**

He should not simply announce a solution because the plot needs it.

For the first game:

**A* baseline → repeated-cost measurement → known flow-field technique → shared navigation → local
steering → destination/prediction problem → influence field → uncertainty/decay → adversarial tests →
real-device constraints → release.**

## 17. Revision order

Do not optimize prose style before continuity is stable.

Recommended revision order:

1. causal correctness;
2. character motivation;
3. relationship progression;
4. timeline/provenance;
5. scene order and cuts;
6. pacing;
7. repetition/exposition;
8. sentence-level style.

## 18. What outside analysis tools are for

External writing analysis should be used as a **second opinion**, not as the author.

At planning stage, our own causal/continuity work is more valuable.

After a substantial first prose sample exists, analytical tools become useful for:

- pacing distribution;
- repetition;
- dialogue balance;
- sentence variation;
- readability;
- character/plot/setting consistency;
- timeline or structural warnings.

They are especially useful after Episode 1 or the first two complete episodes exist, because we can then compare
tool feedback against the intended narrative style rather than reacting to isolated paragraphs.

A report is evidence, not a command. Intentional repetition, slow scenes and unusual sentence rhythms may be
correct.

## 19. Master quality test

A scene should ideally satisfy most of these:

- characters have a reason to be there;
- previous scenes matter;
- future behavior remains plausible;
- the scene changes something or reveals something;
- dialogue sounds like people rather than an outline;
- the world continues beyond the scene;
- the reader can understand more than Nathan when useful;
- no important object appears from nowhere;
- canon characters retain independent lives;
- Nathan does not solve the entire world simply because he knows canon.

The most important global test is:

> Does the story feel as though we are selecting the best moments from a life that genuinely continues,
> rather than inventing each scene only when we need it?
