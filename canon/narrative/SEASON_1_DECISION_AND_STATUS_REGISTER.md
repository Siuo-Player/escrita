## 1. Core premise and world rules

- The story is set in a parallel world in which the Dunphy, Pritchett and Tucker family members are real people with independent private lives. They are not actors performing the episodes Nathan remembers.
- Nathan Callahan has been privately reincarnated into his own childhood. He remembers the *Modern Family* television series from his previous life; the people around him do not know this.
- The remembered episodes are memory labels and reference material, not broadcasts or scripts in the parallel world. A real-world air date is not automatically the in-world date of an event.
- The butterfly effect is cumulative. The original series chronology is an initial reference only. Once Nathan changes timing, access, relationships or information, later predictions that depend on those conditions must be reconsidered.
- In the absence of a confirmed world-difference entry, the researched real-world historical, social, legal, geographic and technical baseline applies. Research uncertainty is not permission to invent a divergence.
- The Callahan family is an original family in this world, independent of the television families.

Authoritative sources: `WORLD_DIFFERENCE_REGISTRY.md` (WD-001–WD-004), `EVENT_REGISTRY.md`, `CALLAHAN_FAMILY.md`.

## 3. Nathan: identity, age, skills and limitations

- Nathan turns **13 on 7 September 2009**; his birthday is the opening date of the season. His prior-life transition is a separate event roughly two weeks earlier.
- He grew up in the Seattle area with Clifford and Heather and relocates with them to Cheviot Hills, Los Angeles, in September 2009 after Clifford accepts a California-based leadership role.
- He is academically strong, especially in mathematics and computing, and already has substantial C++ experience. His technical advantage comes from study, practice, memory and recognizing useful ideas—not unexplained future technology or magical competence.
- He is an experienced youth soccer player and was captain of his Seattle team. In the California team he starts as a new player; leadership and playing time must be earned in that environment.
- His central weaknesses are fear of seeming arrogant, fear of rejection, self-undervaluation and reluctance to initiate unfamiliar friendships. He is not socially incapable: he is more at ease when another person begins the interaction or when the shared activity provides a clear purpose.
- His previous-life memory is unusually detailed for events actually shown in the series, but it does not reveal every off-screen conversation, private motive or medical fact. Memory certainty and prediction certainty are separate.

## 4. School and calendar continuity

- The story opens on **Monday, 7 September 2009**. Nathan turns 13 that day; it is Labor Day, not a normal school day.
- The documented 2009–10 school-calendar baseline places the start of instruction on **Wednesday, 9 September**. Nathan transfers to his new Southern California school on **Wednesday, 16 September**.
- Nathan is in **8th grade**; Alex is in **7th grade**. They share a school campus but not most core classes. Their contact should arise through plausible common spaces, school resources, activities or deliberate encounters.
- Nathan is therefore one school year above Alex throughout the 2009–10 school year. Under DEC-011, at the end of Season 1 Nathan completes 8th grade and enters High School; Alex completes 7th grade and moves into 8th grade. They do not graduate middle school together.
- The recurring weekly rhythm is a continuity aid, not a diary: programming usually Monday/Wednesday/Friday, football training Tuesday/Thursday, a Saturday match or team activity, and lighter Sunday planning. The story should show selected moments and allow ordinary days to pass.
- A scene may move within a school week when there is a concrete narrative reason, but class times, school-day boundaries, holidays and travel must remain plausible.

Sources: `SEASON_1_CONTINUITY.md`, `SEASON_1_RECURRING_SCHEDULES.md`, `OPENING_CALENDAR_AUDIT.md`, DEC-011.

## 5. Nathan and Alex: relationship direction

- Nathan is attracted to Alex first because he recognizes her from his previous-life memories. Alex does not begin with equivalent romantic interest.
- Their first school interaction is practical and initiated by Alex on **18 September 2009**, after she notices a relevant mathematics resource.
- The relationship is **friends before romance**. Its first-season outcome is a growing, credible friendship and mutual trust—not a required confession, official relationship, jealousy triangle or sudden mutual realization.
- Every interaction must be justified by what each person wants, what they know from earlier encounters and why the exchange happens on that particular day. Alex reacts to Nathan's actual behavior, not to inaccessible thoughts.
- Nathan's self-undervaluation should change his behavior in varied, specific ways; it must not become the same repeated apology or internal monologue.
- A later adult-only physical relationship between Nathan and Haley is a permitted future possibility during a genuine separation from Alex, but timing, causes and consequences are not decided. It must not be introduced as consequence-free wish fulfillment.

Sources: `SEASON_1_RELATIONSHIP_PACING.md`, `NARRATIVE_AND_CAUSAL_METHOD.md`, `STORY_DECISIONS_TO_MAKE.md`.

## 6. Nathan's first game: scope and technical progression

- The first major independent project is a deliberately small 2D mobile game, with the important game logic written by Nathan in C++.
- The defining technical idea is shared grid-based **flow-field navigation**, with a separate lightweight player-behavior memory/prediction layer that changes where enemies search or intercept. Prediction and navigation are distinct systems; neither should be described as magic or as a complete AI that knows the future.
- The first implementation begins conventionally, using an A* baseline. Measured repeated path searches motivate the flow-field redesign. Later work adds local steering, spatial partitioning, 2D collision/physics, memory profiling and polish.
- The device baseline is the 2009 iPhone 3GS / iPhone OS 3.1 when the relevant scene establishes that version. The Cortex-A8 includes NEON SIMD; a scalar build must not be described as lacking vector hardware. Use period-appropriate tools and APIs.
- Physical RAM is not the same as a fixed app heap. Do not invent a universal iOS memory partition or warning threshold. Keep app live heap, process footprint and engine-owned active data distinct whenever figures are used.
- Nathan writes and tests the software under Clifford's supervision. Clifford is the adult Standard iPhone Developer Program account holder and performs the contractual/account-holder submission. Nathan is not described as independently holding the paid developer account.

## 7. Remembered family events and intervention policy

- Nathan's remembered source-series plots normally occupy approximately **10–40%** of an episode's plot focus. The real people may occupy much more page time through original everyday events and parallel threads.
- Nathan does not need to attend every event he remembers. Events can unfold outside his field of view and may be experienced or discussed by the families without him.
- Nathan wants to prevent tragedies and serious mistakes, but must first identify exactly what he remembers, the present trigger, whether he has access, what he can credibly say/do, and what the intervention may change.
- Priority is given to credible imminent danger, then serious lasting harm, then major family/relationship damage; ordinary embarrassment and comedy do not automatically require intervention.
- An intervention can fail, be rejected, move the risk elsewhere or change several later events. Do not restore the original episode outcome by force after the story has diverged.

Source: `FOREKNOWLEDGE_INTERVENTION_POLICY.md`, WD-001/WD-002/WD-004.

## 8. Narrative form and episode construction

- Draft prose and dialogue in American English. Preserve period-appropriate vocabulary, technology, communication habits and cultural references.
- Use contemporary serialized-fiction prose with theatrical blocking: bodies move, objects are handled, pauses and reactions carry meaning, and scene rhythm supports comedy or drama.
- Viewpoint can vary when a cutaway reveals a character's independent state or private perspective; each viewpoint must sound and notice things differently. Do not confuse an editorial POV cut with omniscient access inside Nathan's scenes.
- Each episode should normally carry at least **three consequential concurrent plot threads**: one central thread and at least two secondary threads involving other characters. The threads need not intersect in every scene.
- No filler. A scene earns its place through character movement, relationship/state change, a causal setup/payoff, world texture with later relevance, or meaningful variation of tension. Do not inflate prose simply to hit a word count.
- Target episode length is generally **10,000–14,000+ words** for a developed opening episode. This is a planning target, not evidence that a shorter draft is complete.
- The season uses top-down arc design, forward scene construction, backward tracing of later clues/props, and a forward regression pass after changes.
