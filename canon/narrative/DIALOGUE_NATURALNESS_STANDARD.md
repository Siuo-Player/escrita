# Natural Dialogue Standard

Status: ESTABLISHED WRITING STANDARD
Updated: 2026-10-12

## Purpose

Dialogue has been identified as a recurring weakness: exchanges can sound as if every character is explaining the scene, taking turns delivering polished lines, or saying exactly what the outline needs. This document is a production rule, not a claim that a checklist can replace a writer's ear.

The finished story is written in **natural European Portuguese by default**, while preserving the social setting and character identities of Southern California. Planning documents may remain in English. Dialogue should read as a convincing rendering of what these American characters would say, not as literal English syntax translated word for word or as dialogue transplanted from Portugal.

The story may borrow high-level qualities from an ensemble family sitcom—warmth, parallel comic situations, uncomfortable timing and affection expressed through friction—but must not reproduce lines or mechanically imitate the series' exact dialogue patterns. No camera-facing monologues.

## The central rule

**A character speaks to do something, not to explain what the writer knows.**

Before drafting a scene, note privately for each speaker:
- what they want from the other person right now;
- what they are avoiding admitting or asking;
- how much they trust the other person in this specific moment;
- what they know, and what they are guessing;
- what would make them stop, change the subject, or leave.

These intentions can be small: getting a book back, avoiding embarrassment, checking whether a joke landed, gaining another minute with someone, or ending a conversation without seeming rude. They do not need to be stated in the prose.

## Dialogue rules

### 1. Each reply must hear the previous line

Characters respond to the actual words, tone, pause or action just offered—not to the outline's next bullet point. A reply may answer only part of a question, misunderstand it, deflect, challenge an assumption, or ignore it because something else has caught their attention.

If the next line would work just as well regardless of what the previous speaker said, test whether the exchange is merely a pre-written sequence of points.

### 2. Do not give everyone a punchline

Most people are not funny, wise or perfectly articulate on every turn. Let jokes fail, receive a small smile instead of a speech, go unnoticed, or be followed by an ordinary practical response. Some conversations should end because the task is finished. Some should be merely pleasant, slightly awkward, or forgettable in a believable way.

The comic rhythm of an ensemble comes from contrast, timing, priorities and consequences—not from turning every line into a quip.

### 3. Use subtext; do not name the emotional lesson

Avoid dialogue such as “I am afraid of rejection,” “You underestimate yourself,” or “We are becoming friends” unless a character has a specific, credible reason to say it aloud. The reader should often infer emotion from the gap between what someone says and what they do.

A character may claim they are not bothered and then remember a tiny detail later. Someone may offer practical help instead of a sentimental declaration. A person can notice a pattern without diagnosing another person perfectly.

### 4. Give turns different shapes

Real exchanges contain different lengths and levels of completion:
- a short answer followed by a question;
- an explanation that goes too far, then gets cut off;
- a practical correction;
- a pause, a gesture, or a change of subject;
- two people briefly talking past each other;
- a thought abandoned halfway because the speaker hears how it sounds.

Use fragments and interruptions when the situation supports them, not as decoration. Do not fill every silence with ellipses, stammers or stage directions. Normal grammar is also natural.

### 5. Keep exposition out of conversations that do not need it

Characters should not explain shared history, familiar family arrangements or basic canon to each other for the reader's benefit. Let necessary context arrive through a disagreement, a remembered object, an unfinished task, a practical question or a consequence. If an information-heavy line sounds like a paragraph from a project document, rewrite it.

### 6. Dialogue must fit age and relationship

Nathan is thirteen at the opening, not an adult giving a keynote in a child's body. His previous-life experience improves his planning and memory, but attraction, embarrassment, status anxiety and rejection still affect how well he speaks. He can be articulate about a technical problem and clumsy about a personal one in the same scene.

Alex is twelve at the opening. Her intelligence does not make her an omniscient therapist or a constant sarcasm machine. She can be direct and observant, but she also makes wrong assumptions, gets impatient, misses things and has interests beyond Nathan.

No character should sound as though they have read the character bible. The outline may know the emotional arc; the speaker does not.

### 7. Distinguish voices through priorities and tactics

Voice is more than catchphrases, slang or sentence length. It comes from what a person notices, which question they ask first, how they handle uncertainty, what they choose not to say and how they react when a conversation goes badly.

- **Nathan:** hesitant to initiate with unfamiliar peers; once engaged, he can become friendly and talk freely about football or computing. He softens correct answers, underplays praise and sometimes over-explains a technical point because it is safer than speaking about himself. Do not make him apologize in every scene or constantly narrate his own insecurity.
- **Alex:** precise, curious, academically direct and sometimes dry. Her humor should arise from a concrete observation, frustration or the mismatch between what someone said and did. She is not obliged to produce a witty response every time. At first, she talks to Nathan for ordinary reasons and should not immediately behave as if she understands his attraction.
- **Haley:** socially fluent and sensitive to status and group dynamics, but not automatically shallow or infallible. She can use shorthand with family and miss details that matter to someone else.
- **Luke:** concrete, associative and occasionally unexpectedly perceptive. He can be wrong or silly without being written as uniformly stupid.
- **Phil:** warm and eager to connect, often through enthusiasm or play. A joke can be too much, badly timed or simply not land; he should not be performing constantly.
- **Claire:** goal-oriented and direct, particularly when logistics or responsibility are involved. Concern can show up through practical action rather than a tidy emotional speech.
- **Clifford:** economical, pragmatic and used to deciding under constraints. Avoid giving him generic executive speeches.
- **Heather:** attentive to routine and strain, practical and supportive. She can challenge Nathan without turning every conversation into therapy.
- **Recurring peers:** must have goals, blind spots and conversational habits independent of Nathan. Do not make a new friend merely a delivery system for compliments about him.

These are defaults, not rigid dialogue templates. Each character can surprise the reader when the scene gives them a reason.

### 8. Let behaviour carry part of the exchange

A line can be interrupted by an object, task, arrival, competing noise or a practical need. Actions should not translate every emotion into a visible sign (blushing, looking away, swallowing, heart racing). One precise behavioural detail is usually stronger than a stack of reactions.

Do not use gestures to explain a line that is already clear. Use them when they change how the line lands.

### 9. Avoid false naturalism

These patterns often make AI dialogue feel artificial:
- every speaker takes exactly one turn before the other answers;
- every reply is a polished sentence with a clear point;
- characters continually address each other by name;
- repeated “I mean,” “look,” “honestly,” “actually,” trailing dots or rhetorical questions;
- characters summarize their own feelings for the reader;
- instant intimacy, teasing or flirtation before it has been earned;
- one character asks a question only so another can give a long explanation;
- conversations repeat facts the narration has just stated;
- all voices share the same vocabulary, wit and emotional vocabulary;
- the scene ends with a neat lesson or perfect callback.

Naturalness is not a quota of filler words or broken sentences. If removing a quirk makes a line more convincing, remove it.

## Short example: a first practical conversation

The following is a voice test, not immutable canon or finished chapter prose.

**Too explicit:**

— Olá, Alex. Sei que és inteligente e quero ser teu amigo, mas sou tímido e tenho medo de dizer alguma coisa errada.

— Não precisas de ter medo, Nathan. Podemos ser amigos.

The lines announce the emotional outline rather than letting two early acquaintances behave like themselves.

**More grounded:**

— Esse é o *How to Solve It*?

— É.

— A Harris disse para lermos isto tudo?

— Acho que só os capítulos dois e três.

— "Acho" não me tranquiliza muito.

— Não. Pois. Foi o que anotei.

Alex olhou para as páginas marcadas.

— Isso foi tudo no capítulo dois?

— Parte. Não consegui perceber o exercício quatro.

— A sério? Eu ainda nem comecei.

Nathan olhou para ela, sem saber se aquilo era uma piada.

— Queres ver onde fiquei?

— Pode ser.

Ela tirou o caderno da mochila e aproximou-o do livro. Nathan teve de voltar à primeira página para se lembrar de onde tinha começado.


The exchange works only if the scene earns those beats. Do not copy its rhythm mechanically into every interaction. In a later conversation, the same characters should already know each other a little more and should not repeat this exact awkwardness.

## Drafting and revision protocol

1. **Before prose:** define the scene's immediate objective and what changes by its end. Use outline beats as constraints, not dialogue to be expanded line by line.
2. **Draft in small batches:** write one scene, or at most two closely connected scenes, with only the relevant canon/continuity notes. Do not generate a whole 10k–14k episode in one prompt.
3. **Dialogue-only pass:** temporarily read only spoken lines in sequence. Check whether each turn responds to the prior turn, whether the speakers sound distinguishable, and whether any line is secretly explaining the outline.
4. **Read-aloud pass:** read the dialogue aloud, or use ordinary text-to-speech only to catch clumsy rhythm. Synthetic speech quality is not proof of good dialogue. Rewrite lines that are difficult to say naturally or that sound too polished for the speaker.
5. **Independent critic pass:** ask a separate evaluation pass to identify specific suspect lines and explain the problem, without rewriting the whole scene. The writer decides which findings are valid; an AI judge is not the final authority.
6. **Targeted rewrite:** change only the lines or surrounding beats that need work. Compare them with the original scene to make sure the fix did not erase subtext, character voice or useful awkwardness.
7. **Continuity pass:** confirm age, relationship history, school context, who knows what, and the current trust/intimacy level.
8. **Human acceptance:** if the exchange still sounds artificial when read as a reader rather than as an evaluator, it is not finished. Do not mark dialogue “natural” merely because a second model approves it.

## Scene-level dialogue check

Score each item from 1 (fails) to 5 (convincing); a low score prompts a targeted revision, not a generic rewrite.

- **Spoken rhythm:** could a person plausibly say these lines in this situation?
- **Responsiveness:** do replies react to the previous line or action?
- **Voice separation:** could the speakers be distinguished without dialogue tags?
- **Subtext:** does the exchange leave appropriate things unsaid?
- **Age and relationship:** do the speakers sound their age and current level of familiarity?
- **Exposition pressure:** is any line there mainly to tell the reader something both speakers know?
- **Comic restraint:** are jokes and observations allowed to miss or simply not happen?
- **Scene change:** does the conversation alter knowledge, trust, intention or practical circumstances?

Natural dialogue is a result of character-specific intention, context and revision—not a single prompt adjective.
