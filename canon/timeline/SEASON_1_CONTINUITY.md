# Season 1 Continuity Spine — School, Football, Alex and Nathan's Game

Status: ESTABLISHED / reusable continuity baseline
Updated: 2026-10-08

This document is the recurring day-to-day spine for Nathan's first story season. It is meant to be reused
across episodes so school, football, programming, family life and the Nathan/Alex relationship continue to
exist between major canon intersections.

The schedule is a narrative continuity device, not a claim that every bell time or classroom assignment is
historically documented. Individual episodes may move a scene when the story has a concrete reason.

## Calendar foundation

- LAUSD school year opens Wednesday, **9 September 2009**.
- Nathan enters the school on Wednesday, **16 September 2009**.
- Nathan turns 13 on Monday, **21 September 2009**.
- The *Modern Family* pilot remains on Wednesday, **23 September 2009**.
- Nathan is one school year above Alex.
- For the opening school year, Nathan is therefore in **8th grade** and Alex in **7th grade**. This keeps
  them in the same middle-school environment while preserving the later canon fact that Alex graduates
  middle school at the end of Season 2.

The formal narrative Season 1 begins on Nathan's birthday, 21 September, but the school and game routines
are already visible during the 16–20 September prologue.

## Recurring weekly rhythm

The important rule is **rhythm, not repetition**. The same structure keeps the world feeling continuous,
while only two or three school moments need to be shown in any given episode.

| Day | School / afternoon | Evening | Narrative use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Monday** | Normal school day. Homework/study block after school. | **Programming: 1–2 h** | Best day for classroom consequences and a calm technical scene. |
| **Tuesday** | Normal school day. | **Football training: ~1.5–2 h**; light study afterwards. | Team leadership, teammates, physical routine. Little or no serious coding. |
| **Wednesday** | Normal school day; library/lab is often available. | **Programming: 1.5–2.5 h** | Main bridge between school and technology. The **23 Sep pilot** falls here. |
| **Thursday** | Normal school day. | **Football training: ~1.5–2 h**; family evening afterwards. | Recurring team/social material and occasional family interaction. |
| **Friday** | Lighter end-of-week mood; homework can often be finished earlier. | **Programming: 2–3 h** | Longest uninterrupted school-night coding session; good for discoveries and debugging. |
| **Saturday** | **Football match / team activity in the morning.** | **Programming: 2–4 h** or social/family activity. | Biggest combined day for physical, social and technical threads. |
| **Sunday** | Family day; reading and preparation for school. | **Planning + programming: 1–2 h** | Nathan reviews the week, prepares schoolwork and decides what to test next. |

Nathan normally sleeps at a sensible hour. The point is that he is unusually disciplined, not that he works
through every night until dawn.

A realistic sustained development load is therefore roughly **10–14 focused programming hours per week**
during school periods, with more available during holidays.

## School continuity

Nathan's school life should feel like a real part of his routine rather than a staging area for meeting Alex.

### Recurring subject functions

The exact teacher names remain open until needed, but the same subject roles should recur.

**Mathematics**
Nathan routinely finishes the ordinary work early. The useful characterization beat is not simply "Nathan
is smart"; it is that he becomes uncomfortable when his answer sounds like showing off. A teacher can ask
him to explain the reasoning more simply, giving him practice at communicating ability without apologizing
for it.

**Science**
Nathan is strongest when asked to predict, model or design an experiment. He can occasionally give an
answer that goes beyond the expected level, then stop himself when he realizes he is getting too technical
for the room.

**English**
This is where perfectionism becomes visible in a different way. Nathan writes well but can over-edit,
hedge statements and spend too long looking for the "perfect" phrasing. A teacher can praise the clarity
while nudging him to trust his first good answer.

**Social studies**
Nathan knows more context than he is expected to know, but he should not sound like an encyclopedia.
Occasionally he recognizes a future-relevant fact and chooses not to volunteer everything.

**PE / football**
This is where Nathan is most naturally social once the interaction has already started. He talks to
teammates, gives practical instructions, jokes during drills and accepts responsibility without trying to
dominate the room.

**Art / visual work**
This remains a genuine weakness. Nathan can produce something functional, but the gap between his technical
ability and visual ability is useful characterization. He does not secretly become good at art because
the plot needs it.

### Teacher-interaction rule

A classroom scene should normally do at least one of these:

- reveal Nathan's intelligence through the **quality of his reasoning**, not praise;
- reveal his personality through hesitation, self-editing or fear of appearing arrogant;
- establish a relationship with a teacher that can recur later;
- create a problem, idea or observation that affects something outside school.

Teachers should not repeatedly tell the reader that Nathan is a genius. Their reactions should be small:
an extra question, a more difficult problem, a raised eyebrow, an invitation to explain, or a quiet comment
after class.

## Football continuity

Nathan is the captain of his youth/school football group.

The recurring pattern is:

- **Tuesday:** hard training;
- **Thursday:** tactical/technical training;
- **Saturday:** match or team activity;
- other days: football may be mentioned but does not need a full scene.

Football is not a second superpower. It mainly gives Nathan:

- a social identity independent of the Dunphys;
- repeated contact with peers;
- opportunities to lead naturally after someone else has started the interaction;
- a physical outlet that prevents the character from becoming "the boy who only codes."

When Nathan receives praise as captain, his instinct is often to redirect it to the team or assume the coach
is simply encouraging him.

## Nathan and Alex — opening progression

The relationship develops through repeated ordinary contact, not through instant romance.

### 16 September — first sight

Nathan sees Alex at school.

He recognizes her immediately. His first internal response is attraction followed by a reason not to
approach.

He watches from a distance once or twice, then deliberately returns his attention to class.

### 17 September — awareness

Nathan notices Alex again in a library/academic setting.

Alex has not yet become "the girl he talks to"; she is simply a person he keeps noticing.

Nathan interprets every positive possibility pessimistically. If Alex looks in his direction, he is more
likely to think she was looking past him.

### 18 September — first interaction

Alex initiates a short conversation because she needs a book, reference or resource relevant to an advanced
class/problem and notices that Nathan has it.

The interaction should be practical first.

Nathan knows who she is, which makes him more nervous than it should.

He answers correctly but briefly, worries afterwards that he sounded arrogant, and then thinks about the
conversation for much longer than the conversation itself lasted.

### 19–20 September — weekend carry-over

There is no need for a dramatic date. Nathan can simply find himself remembering the conversation while
working on the game or during football.

The important change is that Alex has moved from "someone he notices" to "someone he has actually spoken
to."

### 21 September — birthday

Nathan turns 13.

The birthday does not need to become a giant event. A small family celebration is enough.

Alex may hear about the birthday through school, but her reaction should remain appropriately early in the
relationship: a simple acknowledgment is more believable than sudden intimacy.

### 22 September — second contact

A second short school interaction establishes that the first conversation was not a one-off.

Alex begins to notice that Nathan is smart without behaving as though he knows he is smart.

Nathan begins to learn something even more important: talking to Alex is easier after the first conversation.

### 23 September — pilot parallel day

The pilot's family events unfold in the background.

Nathan is still living his own day.

A useful parallel cut is:

**school interaction / ordinary family-canon event elsewhere / Nathan at his computer that evening.**

He is profiling the game and thinking about why the enemies are spending so much time solving similar
navigation problems.

The pilot does not become an excuse to make Nathan a secret participant in the family reunion.

## First-week scene budget

To keep pacing natural:

- show **one or two classroom moments** in a normal episode;
- show **one football moment** when football is relevant;
- show **one short Alex beat** unless the episode is specifically about their relationship;
- use **one technical scene** when the game arc is active;
- let the remaining school days exist implicitly through references such as "after math," "at training,"
  or "I have to finish this before tomorrow."

This prevents the continuity system from becoming a checklist.

## Nathan's first game — development calendar

The game is built over roughly **7–8 calendar weeks**, with about **6 weeks of substantial technical
development** and the final week or two used for playtesting, packaging and release preparation.

A strong working calendar is:

| Period | Game milestone | Narrative integration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **17–23 Aug** | Concept, map grid, input/rendering, one enemy prototype. | Nathan has already chosen the problem and technology stack before the school arc begins. |
| **24–30 Aug** | Conventional **A*** navigation working; several enemies. | He deliberately builds the normal solution first instead of forcing the clever one. |
| **31 Aug–6 Sep** | Stress test; profiler; repeated pathfinding becomes the main bottleneck. | The problem is discovered through measurement, not authorial exposition. |
| **7–13 Sep** | Re-read/review flow-field material; first shared cost/direction field. | Nathan recognizes the right idea from things he has genuinely studied and from fragmented future memories. |
| **14–20 Sep** | Flow field becomes stable; local steering added; family move + school entry. | Programming scenes alternate naturally with first school days and the first Alex interaction. |
| **21–27 Sep** | Player prediction/influence map; integrate with navigation; tune update frequency. | Birthday on 21 Sep and pilot on 23 Sep happen while the game is actively improving. |
| **28 Sep–4 Oct** | Device tests, balancing, optimization, bug fixes, content cleanup. | School/family life continues while the game approaches a finished vertical slice. |
| **5–11 Oct** | Final playtesting and release preparation. | The project stops being "Nathan's private experiment" and becomes a real finished game. |

### Why this duration works

With Nathan's prior preparation, strong programming ability, a period-appropriate framework and no major
dead-end except the A* → flow-field redesign, roughly **70–100 focused hours** is a good narrative target.

That is fast for a complete game but not absurdly fast for a tiny project whose scope is deliberately narrow.

The important constraint is **scope**:

- one small game;
- few enemy types;
- small maps;
- simple graphics;
- no large content pipeline;
- no network system;
- no sophisticated physics;
- no custom engine.

The impressive part is the AI architecture and the optimization discipline, not the amount of content.

## Backpropagation rules for the whole season

Later events should be allowed to improve earlier scenes without rewriting the basic continuity.

When backpropagating:

1. **Seed later traits early.** A later characteristic should have at least one small earlier
   manifestation.
2. **Technical achievements have prior causes.** A later breakthrough should connect to a book, lesson,
   failed test, observation or earlier experiment.
3. **School interactions accumulate.** A teacher or classmate should remember Nathan based on what he actually
   did before, not because the author needs a convenient relationship.
4. **Alex remembers previous encounters.** The relationship should progress through continuity of small
   interactions rather than repeated first meetings.
5. **Football has consequences.** Teammates, coach and confidence should carry from one episode to the next.
6. **Canon runs in parallel.** Nathan can notice or intersect a canon event, but his own day must still have
   a purpose.
7. **Do not show every routine day.** The schedule is mostly invisible infrastructure; scenes are selected
   where something changes.

The final test for an episode is simple:

> Could the reader place this scene inside the recurring week without feeling that the rest of Nathan's life
> stopped existing?

If yes, the continuity is working.
