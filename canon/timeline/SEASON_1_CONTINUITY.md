# Season 1 Opening Continuity — School, Football, Alex and Nathan's Game

Status: ESTABLISHED / reusable continuity baseline
Updated: 2026-10-14

This document defines the recurring weekly rhythm and the opening two-episode timeline. It is meant to let
the story skip ordinary days without making Nathan's life feel paused between scenes.

The schedule is narrative continuity infrastructure, not a claim that every bell time or classroom assignment
is historically documented. Individual scenes may move within the week when there is a concrete narrative
reason.

## Calendar foundation

- The archived Seattle Public Schools 2009–10 calendar and the LAUSD 2009–10 calendar both place
  the first day of instruction on **Wednesday, 9 September 2009**; see
  [the opening calendar audit](../../research/narrative/OPENING_CALENDAR_AUDIT.md).
- Nathan's story begins on **Monday, 7 September 2009**, his thirteenth birthday and US Labor Day. He is
  still living in the Seattle area; the birthday scene takes place at home, not at school.
- The first school day at his old school is **Wednesday, 9 September 2009** under the current Seattle
  Public Schools calendar baseline.
- Nathan transfers to his new Southern California school on **Wednesday, 16 September 2009**.
- Nathan turns 13 on **Monday, 7 September 2009**.
- The *Modern Family* pilot remains on **Wednesday, 23 September 2009**.
- Nathan is one school year above Alex.
- For the opening school year, Nathan is therefore in **8th grade** and Alex in **7th grade**. They share
  the same middle-school environment while preserving Alex's later middle-school graduation timing.

The story no longer treats 16–20 September as a separate formal prologue. The actual opening begins on 7
September, so the reader first sees Nathan in his old life before the move.

## Opening two-episode structure

The first two episodes deliberately cover a large amount of calendar time. Routine days are compressed;
scenes are reserved for moments that change Nathan's relationships, circumstances or project.

### Episode 1 — Before and After

**Story span: 7–30 September 2009**

The episode opens on **7 September** with Nathan already engaged in programming and still living in his old
city.

The first movement, roughly **7–14 September**, establishes:

- Nathan as a technically advanced young programmer rather than introducing him first through *Modern
  Family*;
- his old school and how teachers react to his intelligence;
- his personality: capable, quiet, careful not to appear arrogant, and much more natural once another person
  starts talking to him;
- his identity as a football captain and his ordinary interactions with teammates;
- the beginning of the mobile game;
- the sudden-to-Nathan news that his father has been promoted into a California-based role;
- the family's compressed preparation and move south.

The move is not treated as a long logistics sequence. The promotion had been quietly negotiated for part of
August, so Clifford and Heather already know the relocation is possible even though Nathan does not. Once the
promotion is confirmed, the company activates relocation support and arranges a temporary rental, movers and
travel. A few strong scenes and time-compressed transitions carry Nathan from his old routine to the new house.

**14 September** is the travel and arrival day: the family reaches the temporary rental and meets Phil, Claire and Luke during move-day unloading. **15 September** is for recovering from travel, unpacking essentials and establishing school-ready routines before Nathan starts on 16 September.

The second movement, roughly **16–23 September**, covers Nathan's first week in the new school:

- first impressions of teachers;
- Alex seen from a distance;
- Nathan repeatedly deciding not to approach her;
- the first practical interaction initiated by Alex;
- first football/social contacts in the new environment where useful;
- Nathan's birthday on **7 September**;
- the Pilot running in parallel on **23 September**.

The Pilot should not interrupt Nathan's own plot. A scene can move from an ordinary school/family moment to
Nathan programming that same evening, with the canon event occurring elsewhere.

The final movement, roughly **24–30 September**, gives the reader a full week of consequences after the
Pilot. On **30 September**, *The Bicycle Thief* can run as the final canon anchor of the episode, again
mostly in parallel with Nathan's own day:

- Alex and Nathan have a second and third ordinary interaction;
- Nathan starts becoming a recognizable person in her day rather than a new classmate;
- new teachers and classmates begin to acquire continuity;
- football and programming continue to alternate;
- Nathan's game moves from "interesting prototype" to a serious, integrated system.

The episode ends with the game clearly alive but unfinished.

### Episode 2 — Finish the Game

**Main story span: 1–15 October 2009; public-launch coda: 23 October 2009**

Episode 2 is not a new beginning. Nathan starts on **1 October** with the same school, teammates, teachers,
family and Alex relationship established by Episode 1.

The main arc is the final development push:

- player prediction/influence behaviour becomes reliable;
- prediction and navigation are integrated cleanly;
- update frequency, caching and local steering are optimized;
- the game is tested on constrained 2009-era hardware;
- small bugs and balance issues are fixed;
- school, football and family scenes continue in parallel;
- somebody outside Nathan's immediate circle can begin to notice that the enemies behave unusually well;
- the game passes Nathan's own technical checks and small peer playability tests; he packages the candidate and submits it for App Store review by **15 October**;
- the planned public launch is **23 October**, after the review interval, without sudden fame.

The game should finish around the **middle or later part of Episode 2**, depending on episode length and how
much space the school/Alex material needs.

The payoff is a small real public release after testing, not instant fame. Nathan can feel proud of shipping something and still notice what he wants to improve next.

## Recurring weekly rhythm

The important rule is **rhythm, not repetition**. The same structure keeps the world feeling continuous,
while only a small number of school moments need to be shown in any given episode.

| Day | School / afternoon | Evening | Narrative use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Monday** | Normal school day. Homework/study. | **Programming: 1–2 h** | Classroom consequences and calm technical work. |
| **Tuesday** | Normal school day. | **Football training: ~1.5–2 h**; light study afterwards. | Team leadership and peer interaction. Little or no serious coding. |
| **Wednesday** | Normal school day; library/lab when useful. | **Programming: 1.5–2.5 h** | Main bridge between school and technology. The **23 Sep Pilot** falls here. |
| **Thursday** | Normal school day. | **Football training: ~1.5–2 h**; family evening afterwards. | Recurring team/social material. |
| **Friday** | Lighter end-of-week mood. | **Programming: 2–3 h** | Longest uninterrupted school-night coding session. |
| **Saturday** | **Football match / team activity in the morning.** | **Programming: 2–4 h** or social/family activity. | Strong day for physical, social and technical threads. |
| **Sunday** | Family day; reading and preparation. | **Planning + programming: 1–2 h** | Nathan reviews the week and decides what to test next. |

Nathan normally sleeps at a sensible hour. The point is that he is unusually disciplined, not that he works
through every night until dawn.

During the first two episodes, roughly **10–14 focused programming hours per school week** is a useful
average, not a ceiling or fixed quota. An ordinary school day normally leaves about **2 focused hours**;
a genuinely free day can provide **up to 8 hours**. The table describes Nathan's usual rhythm, not the
maximum time available. His total target for this small project is **60–85 focused hours over roughly six
weeks**, which fits this schedule. During the late-stage crunch window of **12–15 October**, Nathan may
work until **04:00–05:00** on a particular night if needed; this is an option, not a required daily pattern.
If used, reflect the resulting sleep loss/fatigue in the next day's scene state. Adjust individual days for
concrete competing demands; do not treat late-night work as inherently implausible.

## School continuity

Nathan's school life is a real part of the routine, not a staging area for meeting Alex.

### Old school: 7–14 September

The school week begins on **Wednesday, 9 September** under the current Seattle Public Schools calendar baseline. Monday the 7th is Labor Day; Tuesday the 8th is pre-term/setup time. The opening scenes establish Nathan before the reader meets the Dunphys.

**Teachers:** Nathan routinely finishes difficult work early, asks useful questions, and can explain an
answer beyond the expected level. The characterization comes from how he behaves after being noticed. He
does not enjoy being called exceptional; he is more comfortable with a concrete technical problem than with
praise.

**Mathematics:** a teacher can ask Nathan to explain an elegant solution in a simpler way. Nathan initially
assumes he has overcomplicated it.

**Science:** he is good at prediction, modelling and designing simple experiments. He occasionally starts
to go too far into the technical details and stops himself.

**English:** his perfectionism appears through over-editing and hesitation over wording.

**PE / football:** this is where his social behaviour is most natural. Once teammates talk to him, Nathan
jokes, gives instructions and leads without sounding like he is trying to prove anything.

The old-school material should be selective: a few scenes are enough to establish the baseline that the move
will interrupt.

### New school: 16–30 September

The same characterization continues, but now the reader sees the effects of the move:

- Nathan is new and does not initiate friendships;
- teachers notice his ability independently of his reputation;
- classmates see him as competent before they know him well;
- football provides an easier social entry point;
- Alex becomes a recurring presence through ordinary school interactions.

The key is accumulation. A teacher who speaks to Nathan on 16 September should remember that interaction on
24 September. Alex should not repeatedly meet a "new Nathan" every episode.

### Teacher-interaction rule

A classroom scene should normally do at least one of these:

- reveal Nathan's intelligence through the **quality of his reasoning**, not repeated praise;
- reveal his personality through hesitation, self-editing or fear of appearing arrogant;
- establish a teacher relationship that can recur;
- create an observation, problem or idea that affects something outside school.

Teachers should not repeatedly tell the reader that Nathan is a genius. Their reactions should be small:
an extra question, a harder problem, a raised eyebrow, an invitation to explain, or a quiet comment after
class.

## Football continuity

Nathan is an experienced youth football player and **captain of his team** before the move.

Football remains a stable part of his identity even though the move changes the people around him.

Default rhythm:

- **Tuesday:** hard training;
- **Thursday:** tactical/technical training;
- **Saturday:** match or team activity.

Before the move, these scenes establish his existing peer relationships and leadership. After the move, the
story can show him entering a new football environment without requiring an instant repeat of the old
captain status.

Football is not a second superpower. It gives Nathan:

- a social identity independent of the Dunphys;
- repeated contact with peers;
- opportunities to lead after someone else has opened the interaction;
- a physical outlet that keeps him from becoming "the boy who only codes."

When Nathan receives praise as captain, he tends to redirect it to the team or assume the coach is simply
encouraging him.

## Nathan and Alex — opening progression

The relationship develops through repeated ordinary contact.

### 16 September — first sight

Nathan sees Alex at school.

He recognizes her immediately. Attraction is followed almost instantly by a reason not to approach.

He watches from a distance once or twice, then returns his attention to class.

### 17 September — awareness

Nathan notices Alex again in an academic/library context.

She is not yet "the girl he talks to"; she is simply somebody he keeps noticing.

Because Nathan strongly underestimates himself, even a glance from Alex is easy for him to reinterpret as
meaningless.

### 18 September — first interaction

Alex initiates a short practical conversation because she needs a book, reference or resource connected
to an advanced school problem and notices that Nathan has it.

The interaction is ordinary.

Nathan already knows who she is, which makes him more nervous than the situation deserves.

He answers correctly, but afterwards spends far too long wondering whether he sounded arrogant.

### 19–20 September — carry-over

The weekend does not need a dramatic romantic beat.

Nathan simply remembers the conversation while programming or during football.

Alex has now changed category in his mind: from someone he watches to someone he has actually spoken to.

### 21 September — an ordinary Monday

Nathan's thirteenth birthday was on **7 September**, before the move. There is no second birthday beat
here and no delayed birthday acknowledgment to manufacture a reason for Alex to approach him.

Treat 21 September as a normal school day. Any contact with Alex should follow from their 18 September
conversation and may be brief or absent; the relationship does not need to escalate every time the outline
revisits a date.

### 22 September — second contact

A short interaction establishes that the first conversation was not a one-off.

Alex begins to notice that Nathan is smart without behaving as though he knows he is smart.

Nathan discovers that talking to her is easier after the first conversation.

### 23 September — Pilot parallel

The Pilot's family events occur on their established date.

Nathan continues living his own day.

A useful transition is:

**school interaction → canon event elsewhere → Nathan at his computer that evening.**

He is profiling the game and thinking about why several enemies are spending so much time solving essentially
the same navigation problem.

The Pilot does not become an excuse to insert Nathan into the central family-reunion sequence.

### 24–30 September — the week after

Nathan and Alex now have enough shared history for brief, natural exchanges in corridors, classrooms or the
library.

The interactions should remain small unless an episode is specifically about their relationship.

By the end of September, Nathan is no longer merely "the new kid" to everyone. Some teachers know him,
some classmates recognize him, football gives him a social foothold, and Alex has an emerging reason to talk
to him.

## First-week scene budget

The schedule should remain invisible infrastructure.

For a normal episode:

- **1–2 classroom moments**;
- **1 football moment** when relevant;
- **1 short Alex beat** unless the relationship is the episode's focus;
- **1 technical scene** when the game arc is active;
- the remaining days appear through continuity references and time skips.

A two-episode opening should therefore feel like a lived period of several weeks without requiring a
day-by-day diary.

## Nathan's first game — compressed development calendar

The visible development period runs from **7 September to 15 October 2009**: 39 calendar days inclusive. The full project cycle starts on 3 September with four off-page planning/setup days; App Store review then runs to the working public-launch target of 23 October. It is deliberately faster than the earlier 7–8 week plan because Nathan already knows what he
wants to build, has strong C++ skills, uses an existing 2D framework and has no major failure besides the
A* → flow-field redesign.

| Period | Game milestone | Narrative integration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **7–9 Sep** | A very early runnable build already contains the crude grid, controls, one enemy loop and A* baseline; Nathan checks the simplest one/five-enemy cases and begins measuring the cost of repeated searches. | Opens Episode 1 with Nathan's technical identity and old-school life. |
| **10–13 Sep** | Nathan refines and stress-tests the existing A* baseline with more enemies and a small playable map; repeated independent searches and simple cache/update tweaks reveal scaling limits. | The normal solution is useful, measurable and imperfect. |
| **14–15 Sep** | Move interrupts work; short transition to California. | Technical work continues only in fragments while family logistics dominate. |
| **16–23 Sep** | Nathan applies flow-field material he already studied, builds a shared navigation field and iterates through integration/debugging; further testing refines rather than first discovers the repeated-search cost. | New school, Alex's first interaction and the Pilot run alongside the technical pivot. |
| **24–30 Sep** | Flow field stabilizes; local steering and shared navigation are integrated; first prediction experiments. | Week-after-Pilot school and football material alternates with increasingly focused programming. |
| **1–7 Oct** | Player prediction/influence field becomes reliable; goal selection and navigation connect cleanly. | Episode 2 opens with the project already technically mature. |
| **8–12 Oct** | Simulator profiling, caching, update-frequency reduction and balancing. No physical-device test is established in this interval. | Nathan's perfectionism becomes the main technical pressure. |
| **13–15 Oct** | First explicit iPod touch stress test, remaining defect fixes and regressions, final peer playability check, packaging and App Store submission. | Core development finishes; public availability follows review, with 23 October as the working target. |

### Why this faster duration works

A useful narrative target is roughly **60–85 focused development hours**.

That is aggressive, but the game's scope is intentionally tiny and Nathan is not learning game development from
zero. He already understands C++, has studied the relevant concepts and knows which parts he wants to control.

The work is accelerated by:

- one compact game;
- one or two small maps at first, then a few variants;
- few enemy archetypes;
- simple original geometric art and simple original/generated tones for the first release;
- a period-appropriate 2D framework;
- no custom engine;
- no network system;
- no large physics system;
- no major debugging disaster;
- one meaningful architectural detour: **A* → flow field**.

The technical achievement is therefore the quality of the architecture and the speed at which Nathan takes it
from prototype to polished playable software, not the quantity of content.

## Backpropagation rules

Later development should be allowed to improve earlier scenes without changing their basic purpose.

1. **Seed later traits early.** Perfectionism, self-undervaluation, technical curiosity and leadership each
   receive small early manifestations.
2. **Technical achievements have prior causes.** The flow-field solution traces back to earlier reading,
   measurement and experimentation.
3. **School relationships accumulate.** Teachers and classmates remember what Nathan actually did.
4. **Alex remembers previous encounters.** Each interaction starts from the previous one.
5. **Football has consequences.** Teammates and confidence carry forward.
6. **Canon runs in parallel.** Nathan can intersect canon without becoming its hidden protagonist.
7. **Do not show every routine day.** The calendar exists so the prose can skip them.

The final test is:

> Could the reader place this scene inside the recurring week without feeling that the rest of Nathan's
> life stopped existing?

If yes, the continuity is working.
