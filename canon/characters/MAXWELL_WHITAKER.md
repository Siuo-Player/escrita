## Identity and role

**Age:** 13 in autumn 2009; eighth-grade student in the Southern California school district. Exact birthday is not needed until a scene requires it.  
**Football role:** attacking midfielder / wide substitute in the local team. Minutes and starting status belong to Coach Lance Conley. Nathan's former Seattle captaincy gives him no automatic authority over Maxwell or this squad.  
**Narrative function:** Nathan's first developing peer friendship based on shared sport and ordinary time together. Maxwell has independent ambitions, opinions and limits; he is not a cheering audience for Nathan's ability.

## Family and home life

Maxwell lives with both parents and a younger sister in an ordinary, tidy but busy household. His mother coordinates appointments and school information and is usually the person who confirms rides; his father works a hands-on facilities/maintenance job with occasional early or Saturday shifts. His sister has her own primary-school activities and can take up the family's spare time. They are supportive of football but expect him to do schoolwork, look after his gear and give the family notice rather than assuming someone can drive him at a moment's notice.

His parents attend when schedules permit. The family talks about effort and reliability more readily than talent. Maxwell sometimes interprets this as pressure to earn his place every week, which feeds his desire for meaningful minutes; no one at home is deliberately cruel or dismissive of him. He is not poor or neglected, and his family cannot be used as a convenient obstacle whenever the plot needs him unavailable. Permission and transport for an after-match visit or an evening at Nathan's home must be checked in the timeline.

## Personality, goals and habits

Maxwell is competitive but fair. He wants the coach to see him as a player with a useful role, not simply as a substitute who fills a space. He is proud of his tactical instincts and works to improve them. A strong newcomer makes him alert to competition, but he changes his opinion when the newcomer's choices produce observable benefits for the team.

He is direct and concrete. When unsure, he asks a practical question, watches a drill again, or goes quiet while thinking. His teasing is light and occasional, usually to test whether a new teammate is comfortable; he does not bully people or turn every conversation into a contest. When pleased, he tends to include someone in the next activity rather than make a speech about respect.

He likes routines that make him feel prepared: laying out his kit the evening before a match, checking that his boots and water bottle are in his bag, and replaying one or two moments from a game on the way home. He can brood briefly over a missed chance but is more likely to work on the specific mistake than discuss his feelings at length. He has homework and household chores like other students; football is important, not his whole life.

## Games and media habits

Maxwell is a **casual, competitive player**, not a programmer or a specialist in game design. He plays football and racing games on a shared home console when it is available, and has occasionally tried action/stealth games borrowed from relatives or friends. He likes responsive controls, short attempts with a clear goal, and the ability to see why a run failed. He tends to start playing before reading every instruction and learns through trying an action, then repeating it with a small adjustment. Slow restarts and controls that appear to stop responding irritate him more than technical details such as algorithms or frame rates.

When trying Nathan's prototype, Maxwell's useful perspective is practical: does player movement respond predictably, can he tell whether a guard has blocked a route, and can he understand the immediate goal without Nathan explaining it? He might say that the player felt stuck in a crowded corridor if he actually experiences that situation. He would not identify a flow-field algorithm, prescribe a code optimization, or praise the architecture he cannot see.

## Knowledge boundaries

Maxwell knows only what he personally sees or is told. By the first playtest he can know that Nathan is building a small game and that it has guards, movement, an objective and an exit. He does not know Nathan's previous-life memories, future knowledge or full technical design. He cannot report on iPod performance from a Mac/simulator session; Wesley's later device test is separate.
