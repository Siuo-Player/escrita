## Professional history

Connie grew up in the Los Angeles area in a family that valued practical competence and education without treating grades as a person's entire worth. One parent worked in a public-school office and the other in a technical trade. That combination made her attentive both to how a school actually operates and to the way mathematics appears in practical problems.

She studied mathematics at California State University, Long Beach, completed a California teaching credential in the mid-1990s, and began teaching public-school mathematics soon afterward. By 2009 she has roughly a dozen years of classroom experience, mainly with seventh- and eighth-grade students. She has taught enough mixed-ability classes to know that speed, confidence, and genuine understanding are related but different things.

Her school is a normal public middle school, not an elite gifted academy. Resources and students' home circumstances vary. She uses the materials available to her, adapts tasks where possible, and keeps realistic limits on how much extra individual attention one teacher can provide. She is familiar with the school's optional mathematics-enrichment/competition group, but she is not its sole organizer and cannot promise that every interested student will be accepted or transported.

## Personality and teaching style

- **Precise, patient, and quietly demanding.** She expects students to explain why a method works, not just produce the right answer.
- **Dry, understated humour.** She may make a short observation about an elegant solution, but does not perform cleverness for the class.
- **Not lavish with praise.** She avoids labels such as “genius”; she responds to strong work by asking a harder follow-up, offering a more suitable problem, or suggesting a specific next step.
- **Fair about mistakes.** She distinguishes a conceptual gap from a careless error and lets students revise an explanation when they notice that the first version was too abstract.
- **Structured, not inflexible.** She keeps notes about recurring strengths and problems, plans lessons with clear objectives, and tries not to turn every advanced student into an unpaid classroom assistant.
- **Observant but not omniscient.** She makes cautious inferences from repeated evidence and is willing to be wrong about a student's motivation.

She respects Nathan's mathematical reasoning when he demonstrates it, but she can also notice when he over-explains, skips a step the class needs, or retreats from a correct answer because he fears sounding arrogant. Those observations should stay specific. She does not know his previous life, private memories, full skill ceiling, or social/romantic motives.

## Routine and life outside school

Connie arrives early enough to review the board plan and student work before first period. She uses short written explanations and exit questions to see whether a student understood a concept rather than copied a procedure. Between classes, she handles attendance, student questions, planning and the ordinary interruptions of a public school. She marks work in focused blocks rather than assuming she can respond instantly to every request.

Outside school she keeps a modest home routine, calls her parents most weekends, reads puzzle magazines or logic problems for her own enjoyment, and takes a local walk or easy hike when the school week permits. She is interested in mathematics competitions because they can give a student a deeper problem to pursue, but she is wary of treating competitions as proof of overall worth. Her personal life does not revolve around one promising student; she has colleagues, family commitments, grading and her own need for rest.
