# Character State Continuity Standard

Status: ESTABLISHED WRITING RULE — operational detail is evolving
Updated: 2026-10-12

## Core principle

**No person may act in a scene without a causal state that explains what they could know, want, feel, remember, do and reasonably choose at that exact moment.** Their full background can remain invisible in the prose, but it must exist in the project record.

Creating details for the writer does not mean they must be stated in the story. The prose shows only what is relevant, natural and interesting. The underlying record prevents forgotten traits, sudden motivations, missing skills, inconsistent objects and implausible reactions.

This standard applies to Nathan, canon characters, recurring originals, named minor characters and one-off characters who speak, make a choice, change an outcome or are singled out by narration. An undifferentiated crowd can be represented collectively; once an individual is described as an individual or given agency, create at least a micro-profile.

## Required profile depth

### Tier A — principal / long-running character

Maintain a durable character file with:
- identity and exact/estimated age, confidence and source;
- family/household structure, childhood and formative events relevant to behavior;
- education/work, normal schedules, obligations, resources and geographic constraints;
- goals, fears, defenses, values, contradictions, interests, preferences and dislikes;
- knowledge and skill inventories, including what they know incorrectly or only partially;
- physical/health state, injuries, allergies and limits when established; UNKNOWN is not the same as "none";
- relationships, each person's interpretation of the relationship, and what each knows about the other;
- voice, conversational habits, humor, avoidance strategies, stress behavior and what changes their mind;
- property and object history when relevant;
- a dated life timeline and a scene-state snapshot before every significant appearance;
- unresolved questions, confidence, provenance and dependencies.

### Tier B — recurring secondary / scene-active character

The profile may live inside a cast-bible file or a dedicated character file, but it must cover the same causal categories at a smaller scale. Include meaningful pre-story context, a goal outside the protagonist, habits/voice, relationship history, what they know and a specific recent-past timeline. "Secondary" must not mean "reacts only to Nathan."

When a Tier B character first appears in a scene, the profile should explain why they are there and what they were doing before the scene. If a later chapter gives them more page time, expand the profile before drafting it, not after a contradiction appears.

### Tier C — one-off person with dialogue or causal action

Before the scene is drafted, provide a compact but complete **life-to-this-moment** record:
- who they are and why they are physically there;
- enough family, school/work and recent-history context to make their presence credible;
- their immediate aim, pressure, misunderstanding or constraint;
- what they know and how they learned it;
- their relevant skill/limitation, physical state and relationship to others present;
- what action they take, why it fits their state and what changes afterward.

Do not build a gratuitous twenty-year biography for someone who asks a question at a counter. Do build all the background needed to make that question, tone, timing and decision believable. Detail should be proportional to causal influence, not word count.

### Tier D — anonymous background crowd

The crowd can remain an aggregate if no individual is singled out or takes an action the plot depends on. Do not reuse an unnamed individual as a stable character unless a micro-profile and continuity trail are added before the later appearance.

## Scene-state header — required before prose

Every scene in an episode outline should include or link to a compact internal header:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Scene ID / date / time | Exact date when possible; time window when exact time is unknown |
| Location | Physical location and plausible travel/access constraints |
| Present individuals | Who is physically present, heard remotely or merely mentioned |
| State references | Character file and last snapshot for every present/active individual |
| Opening state | Mood/energy, current objective, immediate pressure, relevant recent event |
| Knowledge boundary | What each person knows, believes, misunderstands and does not know |
| Skills/capabilities | Relevant abilities and any reason not to use them |
| Objects / body state | Possessions, location/condition of props, injuries, allergies or relevant physical limits |
| Causal chain | Prior state → trigger → perception → motive/constraint → choice → result → persistent change |
| Carry-forward | What must be remembered later and which files need updating |

Before a chapter is draft-ready, complete scene-state headers for everyone who will speak, make a meaningful choice or alter the outcome. For brief background presences, a one-line causal record is sufficient if they remain incidental.

## Recent-past rule

The **past few weeks/days must be detailed for every active character**, even if childhood is brief. The recent past supplies immediate context for dialogue: a promise made yesterday, a bad test this morning, a missed ride, an earlier joke, lingering irritation or a change in routine.

For any scene-active character, record at minimum:
1. what they experienced since their previous on-page appearance;
2. what happened off-page that plausibly matters now;
3. who they talked to and what information they received;
4. any changed goal, belief, mood, plan, obligation or relationship;
5. what remains unresolved;
6. the exact information available to them at the scene's opening.

A character does not need to appear on-page for their life to advance, but a consequential off-page change requires a cause and dated state update. If nothing material changed, state that only after checking their schedule and outstanding obligations.

## Skill and knowledge activation audit

Before a character chooses an action, ask:
- What can they already do at this point?
- What tools, time, physical condition and information would the action require?
- Would a competent person with these skills use a better approach?
- If not, what specific constraint, ignorance, fear, social cost, competing objective, false belief or emotional interference explains the choice?
- Has the skill been forgotten by the writer rather than by the character?
- Has the character learned something new on-page, and is that learning recorded?

Do not make a character repeat a beginner's learning arc after they have mastered the capability. Do not have a skill appear without practice, experience, instruction or an explicit source of prior-life knowledge. Conversely, do not suppress a known skill just to manufacture tension: change the challenge or give using the skill a credible cost.

Numeric skill levels are internal tools, not novel terminology. Skill, factual knowledge, ability to explain, retrieval under pressure and willingness to use a skill are distinct states.

## Persisting facts and objects

Record facts that could change behavior or continuity, including:
- fears, triggers, allergies, food preferences and sensory dislikes;
- health status, injuries, fatigue, medication or physical limits when established;
- skills, knowledge, false beliefs and memories;
- promises, secrets, plans, assignments, deadlines and missed obligations;
- relationships, boundaries, trust, resentments, attraction and misunderstandings;
- possessions: owner, origin, current location, condition, who has seen or borrowed them;
- travel and schedule constraints;
- what a character has said, heard or witnessed;
- facts about the world a character believes that may be incomplete or wrong.

Use ESTABLISHED, INFERRED, WORKING, UNKNOWN, NOT APPLICABLE or RETIRED rather than silently filling a gap. UNKNOWN means the story has not settled it; it never means "no allergy", "no family", "no skill" or "nothing happened."

## Causal surprise and backpropagation

A new family moving into the neighborhood can happen abruptly. The story need not show three chapters of the family deciding to move unless viewpoint characters could reasonably witness those decisions and there is a narrative reason to dramatize them.

For people already followed closely, an abrupt consequential choice gets a different treatment:
1. preserve the genuine surprise if the event itself is meant to arrive suddenly;
2. trace the event backward to the person's existing motive, pressure, schedule or unresolved problem;
3. add only earlier behavior that could naturally have been present without making the character implausibly prescient;
4. consider an ordinary background appearance or short intermediate scene if that supplies a needed causal link;
5. update every dependent outline, character file, relationship state, object record and timeline entry;
6. read forward again to ensure the setup did not alter earlier knowledge or contradict a prior scene.

Backpropagation is not permission to fabricate an elaborate hidden plan after the outcome is known. The setup must fit what the character could have thought or done at the time. If no plausible earlier cause exists, consider changing the later event or show a credible external trigger.

## Continuity change protocol

When a new fact changes an old one:
1. create a decision/continuity entry with provenance and confidence;
2. identify dependent characters, scenes, props, schedules, skills and later payoffs;
3. update source-of-truth files before expanding prose;
4. keep a migration note if a change has already appeared on-page;
5. run a forward-regression pass from the earliest affected point;
6. record whether the change is accepted, provisional or rejected.

Never silently delete an established fact merely because it is inconvenient. It can be revised, but the revision has to be intentional and propagated.

## Acceptance checklist for a scene

A scene is not ready if:
- someone acts on knowledge they could not have acquired;
- a familiar person forgets a relevant recent exchange;
- a skill or limitation disappears without cause;
- a prop is in two places, reappears after being lost/damaged, or is used by someone who does not have it;
- a health constraint appears only when needed for a joke or vanishes when inconvenient;
- a character's mood or relationship jumps without an intervening cause;
- a character is present at a time/location their routine or travel cannot support;
- a scene introduces a supposedly pre-existing friendship, promise, hobby or fear with no recorded prior history;
- a backpropagated seed was not propagated to every dependent scene.

The underlying record should usually be richer than the prose. That is what lets the visible story remain selective, natural and causal.
