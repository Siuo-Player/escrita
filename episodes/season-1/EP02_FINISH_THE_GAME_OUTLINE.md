# Season 1, Episode 2 — Finish the Game

Status: DRAFT OUTLINE / expanded working draft
Updated: 2026-10-08

## Story window

**1–15 October 2009**

Length: **long-form; no fixed word-count target**.

The episode should feel like the beginning of normal life after the move. The game is the strongest through-line,
but the episode must contain multiple independent plots.

## Central movement

**Nathan is no longer trying to become settled; he is settled enough to start building something.**

The game that **began on 7 September in Episode 1** reaches its established release point on **15 October**. Episode 2 is the second half of the same continuous project, not a new project.

The emotional plot is quieter:
Nathan and Alex become comfortable enough that their conversations no longer require an excuse.

## Persistent threads

### A — Game
Prediction field → integration → optimization → old-device testing → release.

### B — Nathan + Alex
Academic acquaintance → genuine friendship/interest.

### C — School
Nathan's reputation begins to exist independently of being new.

### D — Football
Nathan earns his place in the new group.

### E — Pierce family
The temporary rental begins to feel like home.

### F — Modern Family AU
*Come Fly with Me* on 7 Oct and *The Incident* on 14 Oct. Show in parallel or use consequences when

### G — independent background

## MOVEMENT I — 1–6 OCTOBER
### Becoming part of the routine

### 1 October

#### Scene 1 — school continuity
Do not reintroduce Nathan.

He knows the building.

He knows where to sit.

A teacher remembers the stronger answer he gave in September.

This immediately tells the reader that the previous episode happened.

#### Scene 2 — Alex
Alex starts a conversation without needing a book or assignment.

The conversation is short.

Nathan almost leaves, then stays.

This is a small victory.

#### Scene 3 — football
Teammate asks Nathan for help on a tactical situation.

Nathan gives a useful answer.

The teammate calls him by name casually.

The social bridge is now real.

#### Scene 4 — game
Prediction field produces a destination that is plausible but too precise.

A guard appears psychic.

Nathan hates it.

He needs uncertainty.

---

### 2–3 October

#### Scene 5 — school
A teacher gives Nathan an optional harder exercise.

Nathan finishes it.

Teacher asks what he does outside school.

Nathan mentions programming.

Nothing dramatic.

Teacher stores the information.

This is the seed for a future academic opportunity.

#### Scene 6 — Alex / observation
Alex notices Nathan reacts strangely when praised.

She asks why.

He gives an evasive answer.

Alex does not push.

This is important because she is beginning to observe him.

#### Scene 7 — family
Short Pierce-family domestic sequence:
- boxes are mostly gone;
- kitchen routine is established;
- Melissa knows where things are;
- David is working longer hours.

The temporary rental begins to have ordinary life.

#### Scene 8 — game
Nathan adds decay to prediction memory.

Old information fades.

The guards stop behaving like psychics.

---

### 4 October — Sunday/world texture

#### Scene 9 — parallel households
Brief independent scenes:
- Claire grocery/household logistics;
- Phil preparing for work/client appointments;
- Jay dealing with business;
- Mitchell/Cameron managing Lily;
- Alex practicing cello;
- Haley dealing with her own social plans.

These can be short.

The purpose is not exposition.

The purpose is to make the world exist when Nathan is not there.

#### Scene 10 — Nathan
Weekly planning.

Nathan writes a realistic to-do list:
- school;
- football;
- prediction tuning;
- device testing.

He crosses out an overly ambitious item.

This is a rare instance where discipline wins over perfectionism.

## MOVEMENT II — 5–9 OCTOBER
### The game and the relationship both start to mature

### 5 October

#### Scene 11 — school
A classmate begins treating Nathan as a reliable person rather than a smart novelty.

#### Scene 12 — Alex
Alex walks with Nathan for part of the way after school.

No excuse.

Silence is not awkward enough to make either of them leave.

Nathan notices.

Then assumes she had nowhere else to go.

#### Scene 13 — game
Full pursuit test.

The player deliberately repeats the same escape route.

The prediction model learns.

One guard cuts the route.

Another checks a different probable exit.

Nathan smiles.

Now the system feels like the game he imagined.

---

### 6 October

#### Scene 14 — football
Competitive teammate and Nathan cooperate successfully.

The teammate earns more playing time.

Nathan is happy for him.

The teammate realizes Nathan is not trying to take his place.

Friendship develops.

#### Scene 15 — school
Teacher mentions a possible club/competition.

Nathan is interested.

He hesitates because joining something new means talking to people.

Alex later makes an offhand comment that it sounds like something he would be good at.

It matters more to him than it should.

#### Scene 16 — game
Nathan compares:
- A* baseline;
- flow field;
- full architecture.

The difference is measurable.

This can be one of the strongest technical scenes.

## MOVEMENT III — 8–11 OCTOBER
### The new normal and the first real weekend

### 8 October

#### Scene 20 — school
Teacher gives Nathan an unexpectedly difficult question.

Nathan answers.

Another student looks impressed.

Nathan becomes uncomfortable.

Teacher changes the subject rather than dwelling on it.

#### Scene 21 — Alex
Alex notices this reaction.

She teases him lightly.

Nathan laughs.

This is one of the first moments of uncomplicated interaction.

#### Scene 22 — game
Prediction has too much historical memory.

Nathan tunes the weighting:
recent behavior matters more than old behavior.

This foreshadows his later understanding of people:
old scripts are not enough.

### 9 October

#### Scene 23 — football
Nathan's teammate asks him to help outside formal practice.

This is the first real invitation.

Nathan says yes without overthinking.

Small but important.

#### Scene 24 — family
Melissa buys/arranges something for the rental.

The house acquires another normal household detail.

No exposition needed.

#### Scene 25 — game
First complete playthrough.

The game works.

Nathan wins the test.

Then finds six things he dislikes.

---

### 10 October — Saturday

#### Scene 26 — football match
Nathan's team plays.

He performs well.

The coach publicly trusts him with a tactical adjustment.

Nathan does not become captain.

He is simply becoming important.

#### Scene 27 — post-match
Teammate invites him to do something afterward.

Nathan hesitates.

Then accepts.

This is probably his biggest social action of the episode.

#### Scene 28 — game
Late afternoon/evening.

Nathan tests the game with 20–24 enemies.

Performance is now acceptable.

The remaining problems are optimization and polish.

#### Scene 29 — Alex echo
Alex does not need to appear.

Nathan simply notices that he is looking forward to talking to her on Monday.

---

### 11 October — Sunday

#### Scene 30 — family / local life
A short family outing.

The rental neighborhood now feels familiar.

Someone mentions how long they will stay.

Nathan realizes they may actually buy here eventually.

Do not resolve the future house yet.

#### Scene 31 — Nathan
Weekly plan.

He sees how much work remains.

Instead of working until 2 a.m., he stops at a reasonable hour.

This shows discipline.

## MOVEMENT IV — 12–15 OCTOBER
### Final push

### 12 October

#### Scene 32 — school pressure
A normal assignment collides with game testing.

Nathan tries to do both perfectly.

He cannot.

#### Scene 33 — parent interaction
David or Melissa notices.

The parent does not tell him to abandon the game.

They tell him to choose what actually matters tonight.

Nathan learns prioritization.

#### Scene 34 — Alex
Alex notices he is tired.

She asks if he slept.

Nathan says yes.

She knows he is lying.

Not because she has magical insight.

Because she has now spent enough time around him.

This is a relationship milestone.

#### Scene 35 — game
Optimization:
- update frequencies;
- caching;
- compact state;
- fewer redundant calculations;
- device profiling.

The story should show the work through decisions rather than code.

---

### 13 October

#### Scene 36 — school
Teacher gives Nathan a small academic opportunity.

Nathan accepts.

He now initiates the "yes" himself.

#### Scene 37 — football
Teammate casually treats him as part of the group.

No ceremony.

That is the point.

#### Scene 38 — game
Old-device test.

The game runs.

One last performance cliff appears.

Nathan fixes it by reducing work, not by demanding more hardware.

#### Scene 39 — Alex
A short conversation.

Nathan makes a joke first.

Alex laughs.

He immediately thinks maybe she was just being polite.

The reader sees the mismatch.

## 15 October — release

### Scene 43 — morning
Nathan is tired but functioning.

School feels normal.

That matters.

The reader should feel how far he has come in one month.

### Scene 44 — teacher
Teacher sees a strong piece of work.

Instead of praise alone, the teacher gives Nathan a harder follow-up.

Nathan likes that more than a compliment.

### Scene 45 — football
Teammate asks him about the weekend.

No longer "the new kid."

Just Nathan.

### Scene 46 — Alex
Alex initiates another conversation.

Nathan stays without searching for an exit.

The conversation contains no academic excuse.

Their relationship is now clearly real even if neither calls it anything.

### Scene 47 — final build
Nathan packages the game.

The release process is deliberately small.

No instant app-store celebrity.

He sends a test/release build to a few people.

One technically inclined tester notices:
"The enemies move differently."

Nathan explains the architecture.

The tester becomes interested.

This is the first seed of later technical recognition.

### Scene 48 — final emotional beat
Nathan watches the finished game.

Several guards move through the level.

He likes it.

Then spots something he would still change.

He closes the profiler.

For tonight.

# Expanded scene architecture — causal / narrative pass

Status: WORKING ADDENDUM
Updated: 2026-10-08

Episode 2 should feel less like "the next set of introductions" and more like a life that has started running
on its own.

## Narrative camera

The episode can cut away from Nathan more often than Episode 1 because the reader now understands the local
world.

Good reasons to leave Nathan:

- show what another character is deciding;
- establish a canon thread in its natural form;
- show the next link in a butterfly chain;
- let Alex/Haley/Claire/Phil have a scene whose value does not depend on Nathan;
- show a domestic action whose consequence will matter later.

Do not cut away simply to tick a character off a schedule.

## 1–6 October — routine becomes infrastructure

The most important change from September is invisible.

People now remember Nathan.

That means:

- teachers address him by name;
- teammates include him in plans;
- Alex can start talking without explanation;
- Claire/Phil can recognize him in public;
- Nathan no longer has to mentally map every room before acting.

### 1 October — first "ordinary" Alex conversation

The conversation should begin without an academic excuse.

Possible natural triggers:

- Alex comments on something from class;
- Nathan mentions a book/project nearby;
- they walk the same corridor;
- Alex asks a trivial question she could answer herself but wants to ask him anyway.

The important thing is that neither character needs to justify why they are talking.

Nathan almost uses the first reasonable exit.

He doesn't.

That is a larger relationship milestone than flirtation.

### 2–3 October — reputation forms

The teacher harder-exercise scene and the football-team scene should mirror each other.

Academic side:
someone now expects Nathan to handle difficult work.

Football side:
someone now expects Nathan to help solve a tactical problem.

Nathan treats both expectations as normal because he is still underestimating his own significance.

The world is beginning to disagree with his self-image.

### Family routine

The Pierce rental should also move from "boxes everywhere" to:

- one preferred breakfast routine;
- known grocery items;
- established laundry/household patterns;
- predictable David work hours;
- Melissa's work rhythm;
- Nathan's fixed desk/coding hours.

These details should emerge in scenes, not become a household-management report.

## 4 October — world texture

The Sunday parallel scenes are an opportunity to establish the larger ensemble without Nathan.

Suggested principle:

**one action, one character, one consequence.**

Examples:

- Claire changes a household plan;
- Phil confirms a client appointment;
- Alex practices cello and puts away a book;
- Haley changes weekend plans;
- David moves a work commitment;
- Melissa schedules a family task.

Most of these do not need immediate payoff.

Their function is to make later coincidences causal rather than authorial.

## 5–6 October — Nathan/Alex without excuses

The relationship should cross an invisible threshold here.

Before:
"we talk because something gives us a reason."

Now:
"we talk because we are comfortable enough to talk."

The reader should notice the change before either character defines it.

Alex also starts to notice Nathan's reaction to praise.

This is useful because she observes something about him that Nathan's remembered canon cannot provide.

That reinforces the central arc:
Nathan knows Alex as a fictional person, but the real Alex is becoming someone he has to learn directly.

## Game progression: 1–9 October

The technical work should be shown as a sequence of problems and measured solutions.

### Stage 1 — prediction instability

The model overcommits when it has too much recent information.

Nathan introduces uncertainty.

### Stage 2 — decay

Old observations become less trustworthy.

The influence field therefore changes over time instead of becoming a permanent route oracle.

### Stage 3 — integration

Prediction selects a plausible destination.

The navigation field moves agents toward it.

Steering keeps the local motion believable.

The systems have distinct responsibilities.

### Stage 4 — adversarial testing

Nathan deliberately tries to break the system:

- repeat the same escape route;
- reverse direction;
- create multiple possible exits;
- increase enemy count;
- force congestion;
- run on weaker hardware.

The important character beat is not that he succeeds.

It is that he expects the system to fail and gets annoyed when it does.

That is perfectionism expressed technically.

## 7 October — Come Fly with Me

Do not make the episode a Nathan recap of the canon.

The canon thread can occupy a substantial portion of the episode's ensemble story because it is already part of
the world.

Nathan's role is primarily parallel.

Potential structure:

- a canon family scene;
- Nathan's ordinary school scene;
- canon continuation;
- Nathan's evening technical breakthrough;
- Alex/family scene;
- a short consequence that does not require Nathan.

This preserves the source episode's identity while allowing the AU's own plot to grow beside it.

## 8–9 October — technical confidence / social uncertainty

Nathan's game is now working well enough that he can become confident in the code.

At the same time he remains uncertain socially.

That contrast should be visible.

A useful scene pair:

**coding:** Nathan changes architecture decisively.

**Alex:** Nathan spends several seconds deciding whether to say goodbye before finally saying it.

This difference keeps him from becoming uniformly "confident."

## 10 October — football + device test

The Saturday match should serve three purposes at once:

1. continue the football relationship;
2. give Nathan a social invitation or ordinary interaction;
3. provide a real-world context for testing the game on hardware.

The third element must have believable provenance.

Nathan should already own, borrow or have access to the device through an established route.

Do not make a new device appear exactly because the outline needs a test.

A small imperfection on real hardware is useful:
the game works, but performance reveals something the development machine hid.

That gives Nathan one final optimization problem.

## 11 October — domestic stability

This is where the temporary rental can quietly cross from "temporary accommodation" to "our house for now."

Do not announce the transition.

Show it:

- someone has a preferred mug;
- groceries are bought from the same shop;
- David knows where his keys are;
- Nathan leaves the computer running because the desk is now his;
- Melissa changes something in a room because it is easier than living out of boxes.

Later, this makes the eventual permanent-house move emotionally meaningful.

## 12–13 October — perfectionism costs time

The game is almost finished.

Nathan keeps finding small things to fix.

His technical choices should now create a non-technical cost:

- less sleep;
- less free time;
- missed opportunity;
- arriving tired;
- declining a social activity.

Alex notices the tiredness.

Nathan interprets her concern as politeness.

She does not necessarily accept that explanation herself.

This creates a tiny divergence between what Nathan thinks and what the reader can infer.

## 14 October — The Incident as a butterfly marker

The canonical episode should still function as its own story.

Nathan should not secretly "cause" the entire canon incident.

Instead, use the event to test the limits of his memory.

A good structure is:

1. show enough canon that the reader understands the family conflict;
2. cut to Nathan doing something unrelated;
3. later let him encounter an outcome or changed timing;
4. Nathan recognizes that the remembered sequence is no longer exact;
5. he initially rationalizes the discrepancy;
6. the reader has already seen the small preceding causes.

This is the first important demonstration that his knowledge of canon is becoming a probability model rather
than a script.

## 15 October — release

The release should remain deliberately modest.

Nathan finishes because he decides that "good enough to release" is more valuable than infinite polishing.

That decision is character development.

Possible final sequence:

1. final playtest;
2. build/package;
3. test on the actual target hardware;
4. send the build to a very small group;
5. receive one technically useful response;
6. make one last correction;
7. send the final build;
8. close the profiler.

The external recognition should be small but real.

Someone noticing that the enemies "move differently" is enough.

The story does not need instant popularity.

## Alex state at the end

The relationship should end the episode one step ahead of where it began, not five steps.

By 15 October:

- they seek each other out sometimes;
- academic excuses are no longer necessary;
- silence is becoming comfortable;
- Alex knows Nathan is unusually hard on himself;
- Nathan knows Alex likes talking to him;
- Nathan still does not confidently conclude that she likes him romantically.

That last uncertainty is useful.

## Revision test

After the first prose pass, ask:

- Is every important meeting caused by something?
- Does every recurring object have a plausible origin?
- Can the reader infer consequences before Nathan does?
- Are the non-Nathan scenes interesting without becoming exposition?
- Has Alex's relationship advanced through accumulated contact rather than authorial declarations?
- Has the game advanced because Nathan solved observed problems rather than because the outline says it must?
- Does the episode still feel like an ensemble *Modern Family* world rather than a Nathan-only universe?

If a scene fails one of these, repair the causal layer before cutting the scene.

## Game continuity

Episode 2 continues the exact game begun in Episode 1.

No reset occurs between episodes.

Carry-over state:

- A* has already exposed the repeated-cost problem.
- Nathan has already adapted the flow-field approach he previously studied.
- shared navigation and local steering are already integrated;
- the remaining technical work is prediction/influence behavior, optimization, hardware constraints, balancing and release.

The final release date remains **15 October 2009**.

When Episode 2 begins on **1 October**, the reader should be able to feel the accumulated weeks of work:
the codebase, terminology, bugs and design decisions all have history.
